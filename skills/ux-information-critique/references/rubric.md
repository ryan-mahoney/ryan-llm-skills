# The Information Page Rubric

Eight dimensions. Score each 1–5. The anchors describe 1, 3, and 5; interpolate for 2 and 4.

The dimensions are ordered deliberately. The first two ask whether this is the *right architecture* — a page built for the wrong kind of reading, or organizing its content on the wrong principle, cannot be fixed by anything below them. Dimensions 3–5 are structural. Dimensions 6–8 are execution. When a low score appears high in the list, weight it heavily and be skeptical of recommending polish further down.

Two cautions govern the whole rubric. First, Hochuli's: layouts can satisfy every rule and still be dull — rule-compliance is necessary, not sufficient, so score the effect, not the checklist. Second, Warde's crystal-goblet ideal (typography as a transparent vessel for the text) is the right target *only* for linear reading; her critics are correct that invisibility is itself a convention, and consultative and selective pages succeed precisely by being visibly structured. Match the standard to the mode.

## 1. Reading-mode fit

*Is the page built for the kind of reading it will actually receive?*

Willberg and Forssman's *Lesetypografie* framework — the foundation of this rubric. The five reading modes, each with a distinct typographic law:

- **Linear** (novels, narrative prose): immersive continuity. Unobtrusive text face, even close setting, roughly 60–70 characters per line, minimal emphasis — nothing that arrests the eye.
- **Informative** (guidebooks, much trade nonfiction): skimming to learn whether and where. Shorter lines (~40–50 characters), short paragraphs, genuinely informative headings, *active* emphasis (bold) that pops during a scan.
- **Differentiating** (scholarship, complex textbooks): slow precise reading of several simultaneous content levels. A full type family (italic, small caps, bold), deep heading hierarchy, generous leading; longer lines tolerable because reading is careful.
- **Consultative** (dictionaries, references, indexes): targeted retrieval. Dense multi-column pages, small but sharply legible type, strongly marked headwords, strict systematic order, heavy navigation apparatus.
- **Selective** (textbooks, cookbooks, manuals): some parts read, others skipped. Unmistakable typographic separation of content levels — core text, instructions, examples, asides — using hierarchy, color, frames, symbols with discipline, so levels never blur.

Most information-bearing books mix a primary and secondary mode. The diagnostic: name the mode(s) the reading job implies, then ask whether *every* architectural choice serves it. A reference set like a novel, or a novel set like a manual, fails regardless of local craft. Lupton's corollary sets the bar for the non-linear modes: one of design's most humane functions is "to help readers *avoid* reading" — a lookup page is measured by how little of it the reader must read.

**Failure signals:** a consultative text in single wide-measure columns with no marked headwords; a selective text whose levels share one style; a linear text broken up by boxes and bold; long-form argument set as fragmented spreads; one design applied to chapters with different jobs.

- **1** — Architecture built for a different mode than the reading job; the reader fights the page to do their task.
- **3** — Primary mode served; the secondary mode (usually lookup or review) unsupported.
- **5** — Every architectural choice traceable to the mode(s) the book actually serves.

## 2. Information structure

*Is the content organized on the right principle, chunked to how it will be used, and free of decorative interruption?*

Two lenses. Wurman's LATCH: information can be organized only by Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, or Hierarchy (magnitude) — and the choice determines what the reader can *do*: an alphabetical list serves lookup, the same list by category serves comparison. The organizing principle must match the reader's top task, and must be detectable from the page itself.

Second, the boxed-matter problem. Sidebars and boxes institutionalize selective reading and earn their place when they carry a genuine content level. But the seductive-details research (Garner; Harp & Mayer) is one of the most replicated findings in instructional design: interesting-but-irrelevant additions — fun facts, anecdotes, decorative images — reliably *reduce* retention and transfer, and hurt most when interspersed mid-argument rather than grouped. Every box must pass three tests: relevance (serves the chapter's goal, not its "appeal"), placement (doesn't interrupt the argument mid-stream), and optionality (typography makes skippability obvious).

**Look for:** an identifiable organizing principle matched to the top task; chunk sizes matched to units of use (a recipe's ingredients are one chunk, its steps another); parallel content structured in parallel; front-loaded sections — conclusion or purpose first; boxes that pass all three tests.

**Failure signals:** organization by the author's convenience (chronology of writing, internal jargon taxonomy) rather than the reader's task; wall-of-text chapters with structure only in the prose; "did you know" boxes interrupting the argument; parallel content structured differently each occurrence; a sequence the reader must reverse-engineer.

- **1** — Structure reflects the author's process, not the reader's task; decorative interruptions throughout.
- **3** — Sound overall order, but chunking uneven and some boxed matter fails relevance or placement.
- **5** — Organizing principle evident and task-matched; every chunk and box justified by use.

## 3. Page architecture

*Do the text block, margins, and grid make a coherent, consistent stage — across the spread, not the page?*

The double-page spread is the unit of design; the spine, not the page edge, is the central axis (Hochuli). The classical reference point is the canon Tschichold documented: on a 2:3 page, margins in the ratio spine:head:fore-edge:foot = 2:3:4:6 — smallest at the spine, largest at the foot, the text block echoing the page's proportions. Modern books deviate constantly and legitimately (Bringhurst himself trusted visual judgment over doctrine), but deviations should look decided: the near-universal constants are spine margin smallest, foot at least as large as head, and margins generous enough to hold thumbs, notes, and the page's breathing room. Where a grid is in play, Müller-Brockmann's standard applies: the grid derives *from* the typography — field depths equal whole numbers of text lines, and every element (text, image, caption) sits on it. Vertical space added in whole- or half-line increments keeps lines backing up across the sheet and aligning across the spread (Bringhurst's baseline rhythm).

**Look for:** consistent text-block placement across the spread; margin asymmetry that looks chosen; images and captions aligned to the same underlying structure as the text; baselines aligning across the gutter; white space distributed at the edges, not trapped between elements.

**Failure signals:** margins near-equal on all four sides (the word-processor default), or foot smaller than head so the block slides down the page; text or figures crowding the gutter; elements aligned to nothing shared; baselines wandering between columns or pages; trapped white space inside the layout while edges are starved.

- **1** — No detectable system; the block sits differently on each page and elements float.
- **3** — A system exists but breaks — figures off-grid, inconsistent sinkage, register lost around insertions.
- **5** — Spread-level coherence: decided margins, shared alignments, held register; deviations read as intent.

## 4. Text setting

*Is the body text set so that sustained reading costs nothing?*

The load-bearing numbers, from sources that rarely disagree. Measure: 45–75 characters per line is satisfactory for a single column, ~66 ideal, 40–50 for multiple columns (Bringhurst); Butterick's outer tolerance is 45–90. Leading: 120–145% of the point size (Butterick), with more lead for longer measures, sans-serif or dark faces, and large x-heights. Word spacing: as tight and even as the face allows — Tschichold's Penguin rule preferred breaking words to loose lines, because uneven spacing (and the rivers it breeds in justified text) costs more than hyphens. Justification requires hyphenation, always (Butterick). Hyphenation has limits: at least two characters left behind and three carried forward, no more than three consecutive hyphenated line-ends (Bringhurst). Paragraphs: indent *or* space between, not both (Butterick); first paragraph after a heading flush left (Penguin rule); no paragraph's last line stranded atop a page (Bringhurst: "never begin a page with the last line of a multi-line paragraph"). The physiological ground for all of it (Hochuli): reading proceeds by saccades between fixations recognizing word *shapes* — whatever damages word shape (loose setting, gappy justification, letterspaced lowercase) taxes every line of the book.

**Look for (count, don't guess):** characters per line in range for the column count and mode; even spacing without rivers; a rag (if ragged) that varies gently without shapes; hyphenation within limits; page bottoms and tops clean of stranded lines; section breaks marked so they survive a page bottom (the Penguin rule: space alone is invisible at a page break — add a mark).

**Failure signals:** 90+ character lines in a trade format; gappy justified lines with rivers; justification without hyphenation; leading visibly tight for the measure; both indents and paragraph spacing; a page opening on a paragraph's last line; a section break falling invisibly at a page bottom.

- **1** — Measure, spacing, or leading outside workable range; reading is physically effortful.
- **3** — Fundamentals sound, marred by recurring faults — rivers, hyphen stacks, stranded lines.
- **5** — Even color, comfortable measure, clean breaks; the setting disappears into the reading.

## 5. Hierarchy and navigation apparatus

*Can the reader always tell where they are, what level they're reading, and how to find what they came for?*

Two halves. Hierarchy: sizes and weights come from a deliberate scale, not ad hoc choices (Bringhurst: "don't compose without a scale"), each heading level distinguishable from its neighbors by a clear signal, levels used consistently, and depth matched to the mode — deep for differentiating texts, shallow for linear ones. The layout's job is to honor and reveal the text's actual structure. Apparatus: running heads earn their space by *changing* — content that aids wayfinding, matched to book type (Chicago's logic: the book's own title is the least useful thing a running head can carry, since the reader already knows what book they hold; chapter and section names, or first/last entries on consultative pages, are what help). Folios present and findable; chapter openers marked by sinkage, no running head, and a drop folio or none (the classical package); the more consultative the mode, the heavier the required apparatus.

**Look for:** heading levels countable at arm's length and consistent across the sample; running heads carrying information that changes; folios positioned for thumbing; openers visibly distinct; cross-references that give a findable target (page or section number, not "see above").

**Failure signals:** two adjacent heading levels distinguishable only side by side; more levels than the content uses meaningfully; running heads repeating the book title on both pages — or absent from a reference work; folios buried at the gutter; headings styled inconsistently between chapters; "see chapter 7" where a page number was available.

- **1** — Structure invisible or misleading; lookup requires reading; no functioning apparatus.
- **3** — Hierarchy legible but weakly differentiated at some levels; apparatus present but generic.
- **5** — Levels instantly distinguishable and consistent; apparatus actively carries the reader to their target.

## 6. Tables and data displays

*Does every display serve its actual comparison — and does every drop of ink carry data?*

Few's division of labor: a table is for looking up individual values and expressing them precisely; a graph is for the shape of the data — patterns, trends, exceptions; if both are needed, show both. Table craft (Few): separate rows and columns with white space first, rules only where space can't do the job and then as thin and light as possible; numbers aligned for comparison down the column, in tabular figures; time runs across, rankings run down; precision no greater than the decision needs. Graph craft (Tufte): maximize the data-ink ratio — erase non-data ink and redundant ink; chartjunk (moiré, heavy grids, decorative dimensionality) is ink that tells the viewer nothing new. The organizing question for any display is Tufte's: *compared to what?* Small multiples — the same design repeated across data slices within the eye's span — answer it directly and beat one overloaded chart. At word scale, a sparkline can put the trend inside the sentence that discusses it.

**Look for:** the display type matching the reader's actual question (lookup → table, shape → graph); columns aligned for the comparison the table exists to support; rules light and few; series comparable without a legend round-trip; consistent scales across displays meant to be compared.

**Failure signals:** a default-styled table imprisoned in a full grid of heavy rules; numbers centered or left-aligned so magnitudes don't line up; a graph where a table was needed (or the reverse); 3D effects, gradient fills, decorated axes; a "compared to what" the reader must assemble across pages; scales that shift between sibling charts.

- **1** — Displays fight their data: heavy chrome, wrong form, comparisons impossible without manual work.
- **3** — Right forms, cluttered execution — default gridlines, legend round-trips, uneven scales.
- **5** — Form matched to question, ink almost entirely data, comparisons pre-assembled for the eye.

## 7. Figure–text integration

*Do words, numbers, and images work as one argument on one surface?*

Tufte's *Beautiful Evidence* argument: segregating evidence by mode — prose here, table there, figure in the back — is a production convenience, not a feature of reasoning; on a well-made page they are intermingled where the argument needs them. The placement rule is old and firm: a figure appears on the page of its first reference or the facing page of the same spread — never across a leaf (Chicago's form: as soon as possible after the reference). Captions make figures self-sufficient, because many readers enter the page through the figures: a caption states what the figure shows and what to see in it. Schriver's taxonomy gives the diagnostic for every figure: its relation to the adjacent prose is redundant, complementary, supplementary, juxtapositional, or stage-setting — a figure that fits none of these is decoration, and the seductive-details research (dimension 2) says decoration in instructional text has negative value, not zero. Same logic for notes, per Tufte's ranking: sidenotes beat footnotes (glanceable without leaving the line), footnotes beat endnotes "but only one step" — the further the annotation from its reference, the higher the tax.

**Look for:** every figure within its reference's spread; captions that would survive alone; labels on the image rather than legend lookups; a nameable Schriver relation for each figure; notes as close to their references as the format allows.

**Failure signals:** "see figure 12" pointing across a page turn; captions that restate the title and nothing else ("Figure 3: Results"); unlabeled axes or series decoded only via a distant legend; decorative stock imagery in an instructional text; endnotes keyed by phrase with no markers in the text; a figure the prose never engages.

- **1** — Evidence exiled: figures far from references, captions empty, notes a chapter away.
- **3** — Figures near their text but weakly bound — thin captions, legend round-trips, unstated relations.
- **5** — Words, numbers, and images composed as one argument; every figure earns and explains its place.

## 8. Emphasis and micro-typographic discipline

*Are the small signals spent sparingly enough to keep their meaning — and is the fine detail sound?*

Emphasis is a scarcity economy: "if everything is emphasized, then nothing is emphasized," and bold and italic are mutually exclusive tools, never stacked (Butterick). The classical assignment fits the reading modes: italic is *integrated* emphasis — invisible until the reading eye arrives, right for linear text (the Penguin rule: italics for emphasis, foreign phrases, titles; avoid bold); bold is *active* emphasis — visible from a distance, right for informative and selective text where scanning is the job. Practical ceiling: two in-text emphasis forms, each with one consistent job; hierarchy beyond that must come from position, size, and space. One signal per meaning — stacked cues (bold + caps + color for one level) are noise (Lupton's "too many signals"). Micro-craft: strings of capitals and small caps letterspaced (Butterick: 5–12%), lowercase never letterspaced without reason; real small caps and real italics, not mechanically faked (Lupton lists scaling, pseudo-italics, and their kin as type crimes); old-style figures in running prose so numerals don't shout, lining/tabular figures in tables; never underline (Butterick). And a still-image accessibility pass: apparent size and contrast of the smallest text asked to work (captions, notes, labels), meaning never carried by color alone in charts and marginalia — flag suspected contrast failures as *verify on a proof* rather than asserting measurements.

**Look for:** each emphasis form with one consistent job; emphasis rare enough to pop; caps and small caps letterspaced; figure styles matched to context; the smallest working text plausibly legible at print size.

**Failure signals:** bold scattered through linear prose; whole emphasized paragraphs; bold-italic-underline stacks; faked small caps or slanted "italics"; unspaced strings of capitals; lining figures shouting inside prose; six-point gray captions doing structural work; chart series distinguished by hue alone.

- **1** — Emphasis inflation and faked forms; the signals have stopped meaning anything.
- **3** — Jobs mostly assigned but leaks — occasional stacks, unspaced caps, inconsistent figures.
- **5** — Every signal scarce, assigned, and consistently spent; the fine detail rewards a loupe.

## Sources

Worth reading directly rather than taking these summaries on trust — the arguments are richer than a rubric can carry, and they disagree in useful ways (Warde's invisibility against Willberg/Forssman's visible structure; Tschichold's classical symmetry against the grid modernists he first led). - Hans Peter Willberg & Friedrich Forssman, *Lesetypografie* — the reading-mode typology this rubric is built on.
- Robert Bringhurst, *The Elements of Typographic Style* — measure, leading, hyphenation, scale; "typography exists to honor content."
- Jan Tschichold, the Penguin Composition Rules and *The Form of the Book* — the craft floor for trade setting; the classical canon of the page.
- Jost Hochuli, *Detail in Typography*; Hochuli & Kinross, *Designing Books* — micro-typography from the physiology of reading; the spread as the unit; rules necessary but not sufficient.
- Matthew Butterick, *Practical Typography* — the working numbers: size, leading, measure, emphasis discipline.
- Ellen Lupton, *Thinking with Type* — hierarchy, type crimes, "helping readers avoid reading."
- Beatrice Warde, "The Crystal Goblet" — the transparency ideal for linear reading, and its critics on why transparency is itself a style.
- Josef Müller-Brockmann, *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* — the grid derived from the typography.
- Richard Saul Wurman, *Information Anxiety* — LATCH: the five organizations of information.
- Karen Schriver, *Dynamics in Document Design* — the word-picture taxonomy; readers blame themselves, so silence is not success.
- Edward Tufte, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*, *Envisioning Information*, *Visual Explanations*, *Beautiful Evidence* — data-ink, layering and separation, small multiples, sparklines, integrated evidence, sidenotes.
- Stephen Few, *Show Me the Numbers* — tables versus graphs; table craft.
- The Chicago Manual of Style — widows/orphans, running heads, figure placement.
- The seductive-details literature (Garner et al.; Harp & Mayer) — why interesting-but-irrelevant additions cost comprehension.

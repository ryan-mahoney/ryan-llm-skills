## Part I: Per-Expert Sections

## 1. Robert Bringhurst — *The Elements of Typographic Style*

### Measure (line length)
- [VERIFIED] Bringhurst's exact text (as reprinted at webtypography.net, which reproduces the book section-by-section): "Anything from 45 to 75 characters is widely regarded as a satisfactory length of line for a single-column page set in a serifed text face in a text size. The 66-character line (counting both letters and spaces) is widely regarded as ideal. For multiple column work, a better average is 40 to 50 characters."
- [LIKELY] He gives ~40 characters as a reasonable working minimum for *justified* text in English; below that, set ragged right. (Consistently quoted in secondary sources; not read in the original during this research.)
- Rule numbering: 2.1.2 "Choose a comfortable measure" [VERIFIED via webtypography.net table of contents].

### Leading
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Rule 2.2.1: "Choose a basic leading that suits the typeface, text and measure." Rule 2.2.2: "Add and delete vertical space in measured intervals" — i.e., vertical space is added in whole- or half-line increments so the page keeps a consistent baseline rhythm.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Bringhurst's own words on the concept: "Vertical space is metered in a different way [to horizontal space]. You must choose not only the overall measure – the depth of the column or page – but also a basic rhythmical unit. This unit is the leading."
- [LIKELY] Common text proportions he cites as normal for books: 9/11, 10/12, 11/13, 12/15 (type size/leading in points) — i.e., roughly 15–30% added lead for continuous text.
- [LIKELY] Conditions demanding *extra* lead (widely and consistently attributed to 2.2.1): longer measures; darker (heavier) faces; sans serif faces (verified via a secondary source that Bringhurst recommends more leading for sans serif than serif); large x-height faces; text peppered with full caps, small caps, superscripts, or lining numerals. I could not fetch the verbatim list; treat the list membership as LIKELY, the sans-serif item as VERIFIED-secondary.
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.3.3: "Add extra lead before and after block quotations."

### Page proportion and textblock placement (Chapter 8, "Shaping the Page")
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Bringhurst treats page shape and textblock shape as a system: "the proportions that are useful for the shapes of pages are equally useful in shaping and positioning the textblock."
- [LIKELY] His often-quoted framing: the page and textblock produce an "antiphonal geometry" that can "bind the reader to the book" or "put the reader's nerve on edge or drive the reader away."
- [VERIFIED, secondary] The classical scheme he documents for a 2:3 page (Tschichold's "golden canon", see §2): spine margin 1/9 of page width, head margin 1/9 of page height, fore-edge 2/9 of width, foot 2/9 of height; textblock therefore 2/3 of the page in both dimensions, and textblock height equals page width.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Bringhurst is not doctrinaire about canons: he "preferred to trust visual judgment in assessing the placement of the type area on the page, instead of following a pre-determined doctrine." His practical constants are: spine margin smallest, foot margin largest, and the two-page spread (not the single page) as the design unit. (The specific margin ordering is [LIKELY]; the spread-as-unit is standard book practice, see Hochuli.)

### Structure, hierarchy, and detail
- [VERIFIED, secondary] "Typography exists to honor content." Also: "Letters have a life and dignity of their own … well chosen words deserve well-chosen letters; these in turn deserve to be set with affection, intelligence, knowledge and skill."
- [VERIFIED, secondary paraphrase] The typographic page should "honor and reveal" the real relationships among elements — headings, subheads, block quotes, notes, captions. (This is inkwell.ie's paraphrase of Bringhurst's chapter 1 principle; the underlying rule is 1.2.1, "Lay out the page so it honors and reveals every element" — exact wording [LIKELY].)
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 3.1.1: "Don't compose without a scale" — sizes should come from a deliberate modular scale, not ad hoc choices.
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.3.1: "Set opening paragraphs flush left." Rule 2.3.2 (quoted): "In continuous text mark all paragraphs after the first with an indent of at least one en."

### Small caps and figures
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 3.2.1: "Use titling figures with full caps, and text figures in all other circumstances" — i.e., old-style (text) figures in running prose; lining (titling) figures only with full capitals.
- [VERIFIED via TOC, quoted] Rule 3.2.2: "For abbreviations and acronyms in the midst of normal text, use spaced small caps."
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.1.6: "Letterspace all strings of capitals and small caps, and all long strings of digits." Rule 2.1.7: "Don't letterspace the lower case without a reason."

### Hyphenation and pagination (rubric-ready specifics)
- [VERIFIED via TOC, quoted] Rule 2.4.1: "At hyphenated line-ends, leave at least two characters behind and take at least three forward."
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.4.3: "Avoid more than three consecutive hyphenated lines."
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.4.8: "Never begin a page with the last line of a multi-line paragraph" (his widow rule).
- [VERIFIED via TOC] Rule 2.1.4: "Use a single word space between sentences."

## 2. Jan Tschichold — Penguin Composition Rules & *The Form of the Book*

### The Penguin Composition Rules (1947) — [VERIFIED: full four-page text read directly from a facsimile PDF]
Written by Tschichold as head of typography at Penguin Books (1947–1949); a four-page booklet for editors and compositors [VERIFIED via Wikipedia]. The actual rules, by section:

**Text composition**
- "All text composition should be as closely word-spaced as possible. As a rule, the spacing should be about a middle space or the thickness of an 'i' in the type size used."
- "Wide spaces should be strictly avoided. Words may be freely broken whenever necessary to avoid wide spacing, as breaking words is less harmful to the appearance of the page than too much space between words."
- All major punctuation (full point, colon, semicolon) followed by the same spacing as the rest of the line — i.e., no double sentence spacing.

**Indenting of paragraphs**
- "The indent of the paragraph should be the em of the fount body."
- Omit the indent on the first paragraph of any text and after a subheading.
- Sections without headings must be separated by space *plus* one or more asterisks — "Without them it is impossible to see whether a part ends at the bottom of a page or not." (A concrete rule against ambiguous section breaks at page bottoms.)

**Punctuation marks and spelling**
- Thin spaces before ? ! : ; if the keyboard allows.
- Smaller fixed spaces between initials (G. B. Shaw) and after abbreviations.
- "Instead of em rules without spaces, use en rules preceded and followed by the word space of the line."
- Ellipsis = three unspaced full points, with word spaces around.
- Omit full points after Mr, Mrs, Dr, St, etc. (abbreviations containing the last letter).
- Single quotes first, doubles within; punctuation inside quotes only when it belongs to the quotation. Long extracts set in small type take no quotation marks.
- Parentheses for explanations/interpolations; square brackets for notes.

**Capitals, small capitals, and italics**
- "Words in capitals must always be letter-spaced. The spacing of the capitals in lines of importance should be very carefully optically equalized." Word spaces in capital/small-capital lines must not exceed an en quad.
- "Use small capitals for running headlines and in contents pages. They must always be slightly letter-spaced to make words legible."
- "Running headlines, unless otherwise stated, should consist of the title of the book on the left-hand page, and the contents of the chapter on the right."
- "Italics are to be used for emphasis, for foreign words and phrases, and for the titles of books, newspapers, and plays." In bibliographies: authors in spaced small caps, titles in italic.

**Figures**
- "Do not mix old style text composition with modern face figures."
- Numbers under 100 spelled out in text matter; figures for sequences of quantities, ages, etc. Elided date ranges (1946–7) joined by an unspaced en rule.

**References and footnotes**
- Asterisk allowed only if footnotes are few and never more than one per page; otherwise superior figures preceded by a thin space.
- "Footnotes should be set two points smaller than the text," first line indented like text paragraphs, with 1–2 points of lead under the last footnote line to restore register.

**Folios**
- Folios "set in the same size and face as the text, and in arabic numerals"; pagination counts from the first leaf; front matter of unknown extent gets lowercase roman numerals; back matter continues arabic numbering.

**The printing of plays / poetry**
- Character names in caps and small caps; stage directions in italics inside square brackets; play running heads carry act and scene numbers.
- Poetry set smaller than prose, leaded, evenly spaced with middle spaces; verse distinguished by first-line indents rather than extra leading, "as it is impossible to see whether the verse ends at the bottom of the page or not."

**Make-up**
- Prescribed order of preliminary pages (half title, frontispiece, title, imprint, dedication, acknowledgements, contents, list of illustrations, list of abbreviations, preface, introduction, errata), then text, then appendix, notes, glossary, bibliography, index. Each begins on a right-hand page (imprint and frontispiece excepted).
- "As a rule, chapter headings should be dropped a few lines" (i.e., sinkage on chapter openers).
- "The preliminary pages should be set in the same face and style as the book itself. Avoid bold faces."
- "The index should be set in two or more columns and in type two points smaller than the text."

### The Van de Graaf canon / classical margin construction — [VERIFIED via secondary sources]
- The canon Tschichold popularized in *The Form of the Book* divides the page into ninths: on a 2:3 page, spine margin = 1/9 page width, fore-edge = 2/9 page width, head = 1/9 page height, foot = 2/9 page height. Margin proportions 2:3:4:6 (spine:head:fore-edge:foot). The textblock is 2/3 of the page in each dimension, its proportions match the page, and **the height of the type area equals the width of the page** — the geometric "proof" of harmony.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Tschichold called the 2:3 construction "the Golden Canon of book page construction as it was used during late Gothic times by the finest of scribes," and stated he "abstracted [it] from manuscripts that are older yet." Van de Graaf's diagonal construction and Rosarivo's ninths give identical results.

### *The Form of the Book* — arguments [VERIFIED via secondary digests unless noted]
- "Perfect typography is more a science than an art and depends on perfect harmony between all of its elements."
- "In typography neither the old style nor a new style matters; quality does."
- Tschichold, the author of *Die neue Typographie* (1928), later repudiated asymmetric modernism *for book work*: the book, read at a fixed distance page after page, is a conservative object whose double-page spread is symmetrical about the spine; novelty of form works against sustained reading. [LIKELY as a summary of his position; his recantation is historically documented.]
- Subtlety within symmetry: "Strictly symmetrical things are rarely beautiful, indeed disturbance of perfect symmetry is one of the requisites for beauty" — and the practical corollary that a title should be optically centered on the *type area*, not the paper width ("The title must not, as happens so frequently, be placed in the center of the paper width"). [LIKELY — quoted in a digest of the book, not read in the original.]
- [UNCERTAIN] Frequently attributed to this book: the claim that comprehensive reading punishes any typography that calls attention to itself ("Typography that cannot be read by everybody is useless" appears in his essays; exact source essay unconfirmed).

## 3. Jost Hochuli — *Detail in Typography*; *Designing Books*

- [VERIFIED, secondary] *Detail in Typography* is organized: The reading process; The letter; The word; The line; Line spacing and the column; The qualities of type. Its subject is micro-typography: "letters, letter-spacing, words, word-spacing, lines, line-spacing, columns."
- [LIKELY] Hochuli coined/introduced the term "micro-typography" in a 1987 lecture-essay.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] He grounds detail decisions in the physiology of reading: eyes move in **saccades** (jumps) between **fixations**, and take in information only during fixations. Word images, not individual letters, are recognized — so anything that damages word-shape (excess letterspacing of lowercase, over-wide word spaces, over-tight setting) damages reading. This is the causal argument behind most micro-typographic rules.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Hochuli goes beyond rule-compliance: he "discusses what makes an aesthetically pleasing layout, and why layouts can still appear dull even after all the 'rules' have been strictly adhered to" — a caution for any rubric: rule-satisfaction is necessary, not sufficient.
- *Designing Books: Practice and Theory* (with Robin Kinross, 1996):
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Explicitly non-dogmatic on the symmetry question that divides Tschichold and the modernists: "argues for a non-dogmatic approach, one open to both traditional and modern, and symmetrical and asymmetrical solutions," chosen by content.
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Key spatial insight: in a book **the spine is the central axis, not the page** — the double-page spread is the unit of design; even an "asymmetrical" layout is judged across the spread.
  - [UNCERTAIN] Specific numeric recommendations from Hochuli (e.g., optimal word space ≈ quarter of an em) circulate but were not confirmed in this research.

## 4. Hans Peter Willberg & Friedrich Forssman — *Lesetypografie* (1997)

**The core of the book — verify status:** the reading-mode typology below was confirmed across two independent German-language secondary sources (a design-school summary and a student monograph), which agree on names, examples, and most numbers. The original book was not directly readable online. Tag: **[VERIFIED via convergent secondary sources]** for the category names and examples; **[LIKELY]** for the exact numeric values (they come from those summaries, not the book itself).

Their premise: typography is judged by the *kind of reading* it must serve; a book is often read in several modes, and the design must accommodate each. The full scheme has **eight categories** — five true reading modes plus three "typography modes" that respond to special situations. Some summaries count only the five reading modes.

### The five reading modes (Lesearten)
1. **Lineares Lesen** (linear reading) — continuous immersive reading, "one thing after another." Examples: novels, narrative prose. Typographic response: unobtrusive serif text face at reading sizes (~8–11/12 pt), closely and evenly set, **60–70 characters per line**, roughly 30–40 lines per page, balanced classical margins, minimal emphasis — nothing that arrests the eye.
2. **Informierendes Lesen** (informative/skimming reading) — diagonal reading to find out quickly whether and where the text has what you need. Examples: newspapers, guidebooks, much trade nonfiction. Response: shorter lines (**~40–50 characters**), short paragraphs, clearly structured units, *active* emphasis (bold/semibold) that pops during a scan, genuinely informative headings.
3. **Differenzierendes Lesen** (differentiating reading) — slow, precise reading of complex texts with several simultaneous content levels. Examples: scientific works, scholarly editions, textbooks. Response: a type family with a full set of variants (italic, small caps, bold), clear multi-level heading hierarchy, adequate leading; lines may run to **~80 characters** because reading is slow and careful.
4. **Konsultierendes Lesen** (consultative/look-up reading) — targeted retrieval: read consultatively until the term is found, then read the entry. Examples: dictionaries, encyclopedias, bibliographies, indexes. Response: small but highly legible type, reduced leading, densely filled pages, **multi-column layouts**, strongly marked keywords/headwords, strict systematic (usually alphabetical) organization, heavy navigation apparatus.
5. **Selektierendes Lesen** (selective reading) — the reader takes some parts and skips others; reading portions linearly after selecting them. Examples: textbooks, cookbooks, manuals, didactic works. Response: unmistakable typographic separation of content levels (core text vs. instructions vs. examples vs. asides) using the full arsenal — hierarchy, color, frames, symbols — applied with discipline so levels never blur.

### The three typography modes
6. **Typografie nach Sinnschritten** (setting by sense-units) — line breaks follow units of meaning rather than justification; for primers, beginning readers, foreign-language teaching; cannot be automated.
7. **Aktivierende Typografie** (activating typography) — typography that must *provoke* reading among reluctant readers (magazines, schoolbooks for weak readers, promotional matter); "no typographic restrictions," attention-getting is the job.
8. **Inszenierende Typografie** (staging/interpretive typography) — typography that interprets or dramatizes the text (fine editions, display work); legitimate when it supports content, not as decoration.

**Rubric significance:** this framework says the *first* diagnostic question about any information page is "which reading mode(s) is this page for, and does every typographic decision serve that mode?" A reference layout set like a novel, or a novel set like a manual, fails regardless of local craft.

- [LIKELY] Willberg/Forssman also distinguish **integrierende Auszeichnung** (integrated emphasis — italic; invisible until the reading eye arrives at it, serving linear reading) from **aktive Auszeichnung** (active emphasis — bold, color; visible from a distance, serving informative/selective reading). The German terms are consistently attributed to German typographic teaching in this tradition; direct confirmation in *Lesetypografie* was not obtained.

## 5. Matthew Butterick — *Practical Typography* (practicaltypography.com)

All items below **[VERIFIED]** — fetched directly from the site's "Summary of key rules" and "Bold or italic" pages.

**The four most important choices:** "The four most important typographic considerations for body text are point size, line spacing, line length, and font."
- **Point size:** "10–12 points in printed documents, 15–25 pixels on the web."
- **Line spacing:** "120–145% of the point size."
- **Line length:** "The average line length should be 45–90 characters (including spaces)."

**His most emphatic rules (selection, near-verbatim):**
- "Use curly quotation marks, not straight ones."
- "Put only one space between sentences."
- "Never underline, except perhaps for web links."
- All caps "are fine for less than one line" of text.
- "Use 5–12% extra letterspacing with all caps and small caps."
- Don't fake small caps; use them only if the font provides real ones.
- First-line indents 1–4× the point size; or 4–10 points of space between paragraphs — "but don't use both."
- "Always use hyphenation with justified text."
- Use centered text sparingly; avoid goofy, monospaced, and default system fonts.
- Limit exclamation points to one per document longer than three pages.

**On emphasis (Bold or italic):**
- "Bold or italic — think of them as mutually exclusive."
- "Use bold and italic as little as possible. They are tools for emphasis. But if everything is emphasized, then nothing is emphasized."
- Emphasizing whole paragraphs "wears down your readers' retinas and their patience"; underlined bold and bold italic are "terrible ideas."
- With a serif font: italic for gentle emphasis, bold for heavier. With sans serif: "skip italic and use bold for emphasis."

## 6. Ellen Lupton — *Thinking with Type*

- [VERIFIED, secondary] The book is structured in three parts: **Letter, Text, Grid** — covering typefaces/letterforms, typesetting (spacing, alignment, hierarchy), and page architecture.
- **Type crimes** (her recurring device: annotated examples of misuse):
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Horizontal/vertical scaling ("the proportions of the letters have been digitally distorted in order to create wider or narrower letters").
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Pseudo italics ("the wide ungainly forms of the mechanically skewed letters look forced and unnatural — italics are not slanted letters").
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Letterforms too small for their design ("some typefaces that work well at large sizes look too fragile when reduced").
  - [LIKELY] Other crimes consistently cited from the book: pseudo small caps and pseudo bold; "dumb" (straight) quotation marks and hatch marks used as apostrophes; poorly shaped rag; stacked lowercase letters in vertical settings; "too many signals" — using multiple redundant cues (bold + underline + caps, etc.) for one level of hierarchy.

- **Hierarchy:**
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] "Complex content requires a deeply layered hierarchy," and "if a hierarchy is clearly organized, users are more likely to apply it consistently."

## 7. Beatrice Warde — "The Crystal Goblet"

- [VERIFIED via Wikipedia] Delivered as the speech "Printing Should Be Invisible" to the British Typographers' Guild, 7 October 1930; published as a pamphlet (1932, 1937) and in *The Crystal Goblet: Sixteen Essays on Typography* (1955).
- **The argument** [VERIFIED via Wikipedia summary]: choose between two wine vessels — a solid-gold ornamented goblet and a crystal-clear glass. The person who chooses crystal is the true wine lover, "because everything about it is calculated to reveal rather than to hide the beautiful thing which it was meant to contain." Typography, likewise, should be a transparent vehicle for the author's thought; the right question is "What must it do?" not "How should it look?" She claims "all good typography is modernist" in this functional sense.
- [LIKELY] Famous window formulation (in the essay but not fetched verbatim): the book typographer's job is to erect "a window between the reader inside the room and that landscape which is the author's words," and printing should be a "crystalline" pane, not stained glass that shows itself off.
- **Limits and criticisms** [VERIFIED that the criticism literature exists; positions summarized from abstracts]:
  - The scholarly article "Reconsidering 'The Crystal Goblet': The Underpinnings of Typographic Convention" (*Design and Culture* 2(3)) argues the essay "perpetuates assumptions that frame typography's cultural complicity and social constriction in terms of nature and purpose" — i.e., it naturalizes one historical convention as universal truth.
  - Standard critiques: typography is never actually invisible or neutral — every choice signals; "transparency" is itself a style (the style Warde's circle preferred); the argument holds best for immersive linear book reading and fails for display, advertising, expressive, and much information-rich work.
  - [VERIFIED existence; content LIKELY] Butterick's essay "Drowning the Crystal Goblet" (practicaltypography.com) argues the essay is over-taught and misused as cover for timid, unconsidered typography.
- **Rubric significance:** Warde defines the target for *linear-reading* surfaces (Willberg/Forssman mode 1) — but the criticisms warn against applying "invisibility" as the standard for consultative, selective, or activating surfaces.

## 8. Josef Müller-Brockmann — *Grid Systems in Graphic Design* (1961/1981)

- **Rationale — verbatim credo** [VERIFIED via multiple secondary reproductions of the book's text]:
  - "The grid system is an aid, not a guarantee. It permits a number of possible uses and each designer can look for a solution appropriate to his personal style."
  - "Working with the grid system means submitting to laws of universal validity."
  - "The use of the grid system implies: the will to systematize, to clarify; the will to penetrate to the essentials, to concentrate; the will to cultivate objectivity instead of subjectivity; the will to rationalize the creative and technical production processes; the will to integrate elements of color, form, and material; the will to achieve architectural dominion over surface and space; the will to adopt a positive, forward-looking attitude."
  - "The reduction of the number of visual elements used and their incorporation in a grid system creates a sense of compact planning, intelligibility and clarity, and suggests orderliness of design."
- **Grid construction** [VERIFIED in outline via secondary sources; details LIKELY]: the type area is divided into a modular grid — columns crossed by rows producing fields (cells) separated by gutters; the grid is derived from the typography (the baseline grid and the chosen leading), not imposed on it; field depth should equal a whole number of lines of text; all elements — type, images, captions — align to fields. Worked examples in the book run from 8-field to 32-field grids.
- **Numeric readability guidance** [VERIFIED, secondary]: he cites the typographers' rule of **7 words per line** as comfortable, working with roughly **7–10 words per line** for sustained text, and judging column width by legibility at a normal reading distance of **30–35 cm**.
- **Rubric significance:** the diagnostic is not "is there a grid?" but "do the alignments betray a consistent underlying structure, and does the structure derive from the text's leading and hierarchy?"

## 9. Richard Saul Wurman — LATCH; *Information Anxiety*

- **LATCH** [VERIFIED via multiple secondary sources]: information can be organized in only five ways — **Location, Alphabet, Time, Category, Hierarchy** (hierarchy = ordering by magnitude/continuum: smallest-largest, cheapest-priciest, etc.). Introduced in *Information Anxiety* (1989); also called the "Five Hat Racks." His claim, near-verbatim: "Information may be infinite, however … the organization of information is finite as it can only be organized by LATCH."
- [LIKELY] Corollary he presses: the *choice* of organizing principle largely determines what the reader can do with the information (an alphabetical drug list serves lookup; the same list by category serves comparison), so the organizing scheme must match the top task.
- **Information Anxiety core claims:**
  - [VERIFIED, secondary, verbatim] "Information anxiety is produced by the ever-widening gap between what we understand and what we think we should understand."
  - [LIKELY] Related claims consistently attributed: most of what is called information is really "non-information" — data without structure; understanding, not access, is the bottleneck; "a fact is only meaningful when it is related to another fact" (exact wording unconfirmed).
- [VERIFIED via Wikipedia-sourced search result] Wurman coined the term "information architecture" and founded the TED conference.

## 10. Karen Schriver — *Dynamics in Document Design* (1997)

- **Taxonomy of word-picture relationships** [VERIFIED via multiple convergent secondary sources — five relationships]:
  1. **Redundant** — "substantially identical content appearing visually and verbally"; each mode tells the same story; useful for reinforcement, risky for boredom/condescension.
  2. **Complementary** — "different content visually and verbally, in which both modes are needed in order to understand the key ideas"; together the two render the idea more fully than either alone.
  3. **Supplementary** — one mode dominates and carries the main content; the other "shores up and elaborates" the dominant mode's points.
  4. **Juxtapositional** — meaning arises from the clash/tension between what the words say and what the image shows; "without these clashing ideas, the message cannot be conveyed."
  5. **Stage-setting** — "one medium forecasts or previews" content to come, supplying a framework in which the other mode is understood (e.g., an opening image establishing a chapter's theme).
  Rubric use: for every figure, ask which relationship it holds with its adjacent prose — a figure that is merely decorative fits none of the functional categories, and a caption should make the intended relationship explicit.
- **The "blame" study** [VERIFIED via review sources]: analyzing usability-test videotapes, Schriver found that when documents mislead readers, **readers most often blame themselves** rather than the document ("many people blame themselves when they have difficulty using their VCRs or telephone answering machines"). Consequence for critique: absence of complaints is not evidence of a working design.
- [VERIFIED, secondary, general] The book is described as "one of the first research-based portraits of what readers need from documents," treating the reader as someone interpreting prose and graphics together "while responding to them aesthetically and emotionally" — design quality measurably shapes readers' judgments of content and of the issuing organization.
- [LIKELY] Her methodological triad for knowing readers: classification-driven, intuition-driven, and feedback-driven audience analysis, with feedback-driven (testing with real readers) the most reliable. (Consistently cited; not verified verbatim in this research.)

## 11. Edward Tufte — the four books

### *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* (1983)
- [VERIFIED, secondary, near-verbatim] **Data-ink**: "the non-erasable core of a graphic, the non-redundant ink arranged in response to variation in the numbers represented." **Data-ink ratio** = data-ink ÷ total ink used in the graphic. The theory of data graphics: maximize the data-ink ratio, within reason; erase non-data-ink; erase redundant data-ink.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] **Chartjunk**: "ink that does not tell the viewer anything new" — moiré vibration, heavy grids, self-promoting decoration ("ducks").
- [LIKELY] Small multiples introduced here and elaborated in later books; his often-quoted formulation: "At the heart of quantitative reasoning is a single question: *Compared to what?* Small multiple designs … answer directly by visually enforcing comparisons of changes, of the differences among objects, of the scope of alternatives." (Widely reproduced; not fetched verbatim.)

### *Envisioning Information* (1990)
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Chapter topics: escaping flatland; micro/macro readings; layering and separation; small multiples; color and information; narratives of space and time.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] **Layering and separation**: "one of the most powerful techniques for reducing noise and enriching the content of a design … visually stratifying various aspects of the data." The **1 + 1 = 3 effect** (credited by Tufte to Josef Albers): two elements placed together generate a third visual event — the interstitial negative space — so every added mark or rule multiplies clutter beyond itself. Heavy boxes, rules, and outlines are the classic offenders.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] **Small multiples**: "data slices are positioned within the eye-span, so that viewers make comparisons at a glance — uninterrupted visual reasoning" — the same design structure repeated for every slice, differing only in data.

### *Visual Explanations* (1997)
- [LIKELY] Core additions: quantities must carry into explanations of cause and effect (John Snow's cholera map, the Challenger charts); the "smallest effective difference" principle — make visual distinctions as subtle as possible while still clear. (Consistently attributed; not verified in this research pass.)

### *Beautiful Evidence* (2006)
- [VERIFIED, secondary] **Sparklines**: "intense, simple, word-sized graphics" — data graphics at the resolution of typography, embeddable in a line of text or a table cell.
- [VERIFIED, secondary] Contains the chapter "Words, Numbers, Images — Together": the integration argument — the segregation of evidence by mode (prose here, table there, figure elsewhere) is an artifact of production convenience, not of reasoning; on an evidence page, words, numbers, and images should be intermingled wherever the argument needs them.
- [LIKELY] The six **Fundamental Principles of Analytical Design**: (1) show comparisons, contrasts, differences; (2) show causality, mechanism, systematic structure; (3) show multivariate data; (4) completely integrate words, numbers, images, diagrams; (5) thoroughly describe the evidence — provide a detailed title, indicate the authors and sponsors, document the data sources, show complete measurement scales, point out relevant issues ("documentation"); (6) content counts most of all. (This list is among the most-quoted passages in the literature; treat wording as close paraphrase until checked against the book.)
- **Sidenotes practice** [VERIFIED — fetched from Tufte's own site]: see thematic section on Notes below.

## 12. Stephen Few — *Show Me the Numbers* (2004/2012)

- **When tables beat graphs** [VERIFIED — from Few's own Perceptual Edge white papers]: "A table works best when it is used to look up individual values or the values must be expressed precisely." "A graph works best when the message is contained in the shape of the data (patterns, trends, exceptions to the norm) or entire sets of values must be compared." If both needs exist, show both.
- **Table design rules** [VERIFIED via secondary summaries of the book, except as noted]:
  - Delineate rows and columns with **white space first**; use rules/gridlines only when white space cannot do the job, and then keep them "as thin and light as possible." Light fill shading beats gridlines when stronger delineation is needed.
  - [VERIFIED, secondary] Arrange time series across columns (left→right); arrange rankings top→bottom.
  - [LIKELY] Row height roughly double the text size for comfortable scanning (attributed in one summary; unconfirmed in the book).
  - [LIKELY, consistently attributed] Right-align numbers on a common decimal position so magnitudes can be compared down a column; left-align text; align column headers with their column's content; use tabular (fixed-width, lining) numerals so digits form columns; keep precision consistent and no greater than needed.
- [LIKELY] His overarching stance mirrors Tufte with a practitioner's specificity: every pixel of non-data ink must earn its place; visual components (grids, fills, borders) are supporting cast, never featured players.

# Part II: Thematic Sections

## T1. Composition standards

**Widows and orphans**
- [VERIFIED via Wikipedia and trade sources] Definitions per the Chicago Manual of Style and Bringhurst: a **widow** is a paragraph-*ending* line that falls at the top of the following page or column; an **orphan** is a paragraph-*opening* line stranded alone at the foot of a page or column. Caveat for a rubric: "the pairing of the two terms with their definitions has no consistent standard across the industry; some sources use the opposite meanings" — so a rubric should define its terms, not assume.
- [VERIFIED via Wikipedia summary] Standards: CMoS 15th ed. (2003) said an edited page "should have neither widows nor orphans, even if their elimination leaves blank space at the bottom of the page"; the 16th ed. (2011) relaxed this, accepting a page that ends with the first line of a new paragraph (orphans tolerated; widows still condemned). Bringhurst 2.4.8 [VERIFIED]: "Never begin a page with the last line of a multi-line paragraph."
- [LIKELY] A "runt" (very short last line of a paragraph — a single word or part of one) is a separate, lesser fault; a widow that is also a runt is the worst case.

**Rivers**
- [LIKELY, uncontested definition] Rivers are accidental vertical channels of white space running through several lines of set text, produced by loose, coinciding word spaces — chiefly in justified setting at short measures. The cures follow directly from verified rules: tighter word spacing (Penguin: "as closely word-spaced as possible" [VERIFIED]), free hyphenation (Penguin: breaking words "is less harmful … than too much space between words" [VERIFIED]), adequate measure, or ragged setting.

**Justified vs. ragged-right in book work**
- [LIKELY] Justified setting remains the default for continuous book prose in the trade; ragged right is the norm where measures are short (multi-column, sidebars, captions, narrow formats) and increasingly accepted generally.
- [VERIFIED] Bringhurst rule 2.1.3: "Set ragged if ragged setting suits the text and page"; [LIKELY] his justification threshold: below ~40 characters per line, justification cannot be maintained without gaps — set ragged.
- [VERIFIED] Butterick: "Always use hyphenation with justified text" (justification without hyphenation is the classic amateur tell).
- [LIKELY] A good rag is itself a craft standard: gently varying line-ends, no jarring long/short alternation, no accidental shapes — Lupton lists a "poorly shaped rag" among type crimes [LIKELY].

**Hyphenation limits**
- [VERIFIED] Bringhurst 2.4.1: at hyphenated line-ends "leave at least two characters behind and take at least three forward"; 2.4.3: "avoid more than three consecutive hyphenated lines."
- [LIKELY] Chicago and most house styles state the same three-consecutive-hyphens ceiling; many also ban hyphenating the last word of a paragraph, page, or a proper name.

**Letterspacing norms**
- [VERIFIED] Penguin rules: "Words in capitals must always be letter-spaced," optically equalized; small caps "always slightly letter-spaced."
- [VERIFIED] Bringhurst 2.1.6/2.1.7: letterspace strings of caps, small caps, and long digit strings; "don't letterspace the lower case without a reason."
- [VERIFIED] Butterick's number: "5–12% extra letterspacing with all caps and small caps."
- [LIKELY] The Frederic Goudy quip enforcing the lowercase rule — "Anyone who would letterspace lower case would steal sheep" — is real but exists in several variants (originally reportedly about blackletter); treat as anecdote, not source.

## T2. Navigation apparatus

**Running heads/feet and folios**
- [VERIFIED — Penguin rules] Default book convention per Tschichold: "Running headlines, unless otherwise stated, should consist of the title of the book on the left-hand page, and the contents of the chapter on the right," set in slightly letterspaced small caps. Folios in the text size and face, arabic numerals; roman numerals for front matter; back matter continues the arabic sequence.
- [VERIFIED via CMoS-derived sources] The governing principle: "the choice of running heads is governed chiefly by the structure and nature of the book"; usual combinations by book type — fiction: author (verso) / book title (recto); nonfiction monograph: chapter title recto with part or chapter on verso; cookbooks, manuals, reference: chapter title (verso) / current subheading (recto); dictionaries/encyclopedias: first and last headwords on the page. [LIKELY] CMoS considers the book title the *least* useful running-head content, since the reader already knows what book they hold — running heads should carry information that changes and thus aids wayfinding.
- [VERIFIED via CMoS-derived sources] Display pages (chapter openers, part titles, blanks, and pages consisting wholly of illustrations/tables in some styles) carry **no running head**; chapter openers take a **drop folio** (page number moved to the foot) or no folio.
- Rubric tie-in [inference from Willberg/Forssman]: the more consultative/selective the reading mode, the heavier the required apparatus (dictionary-style headwords, section numbers in heads, thumb tabs); a linear novel needs almost none.

**Chapter opening conventions**
- [VERIFIED — Penguin rules] "As a rule, chapter headings should be dropped a few lines" (sinkage); major divisions begin on a right-hand page (recto), with stated exceptions.
- [VERIFIED via trade sources] Modern statement of the same package: chapter opener = sinkage (extra white space, commonly around one-third of the page), chapter number/title, no running head, drop folio or none; optionally a drop cap ("extending two or three lines") and/or first line in small caps as "a typographic bridge" into the text.
- [VERIFIED] First paragraph after any heading set flush left, no indent (Penguin; Bringhurst 2.3.1).

## T3. Notes: footnotes vs. endnotes vs. sidenotes

- **Tufte's position** [VERIFIED — fetched from edwardtufte.com]: he prefers **sidenotes** because the reader can assess them "out of the corner of [the] eye" without breaking reading flow; footnotes force "jumping to the bottom of the page" and a visual re-adjustment; and footnotes are only "one step better than endnotes, but only one step." His own books use sidenotes/margin material extensively: "Quite often I use sidenotes as detailed annotation or as a sidebar extension … to support the main text," holding material "that would break up the main text if included there" — and sidenote content should be "almost entirely content-driven, rather than design-driven."
- **The classical spec for footnotes** [VERIFIED — Penguin rules]: two points smaller than text; asterisk permitted only when notes are rare (≤1/page), superior figures otherwise; first lines indented like text paragraphs; leading adjusted to keep page register.
- **The tradeoff space** [LIKELY — standard editorial consensus, consistent across CMoS-adjacent sources]: footnotes give same-page access and suit works meant for differentiating/consultative reading (scholarship, reference), at the cost of gray, bottom-heavy pages and complex make-up; endnotes yield clean pages and are the trade-publishing default, at the cost of a two-handed reading experience readers of serious nonfiction routinely complain about, made worse when endnotes are unnumbered or keyed by phrase; sidenotes/margin notes offer the best proximity and glanceability (Tufte's argument) but demand a wide fore-edge margin — a page-architecture commitment (single wide-margin column) that most trade formats refuse to make.
- [UNCERTAIN] Bringhurst's specific ranking of note placements in *Elements* (he discusses notes in the chapter on structural elements and is often said to favor placing notes in the side margin when the format allows); not confirmed this pass — verify before attributing.

## T4. Captions and figure placement

- **Placement relative to reference** [VERIFIED via CMoS-derived guidance]: a figure appears "as soon as possible after your first reference" to it; it may precede the reference only when both fall on the same page; equivalently, place it immediately after the paragraph of first mention (same page or next) or just before the mention on the same page. The book-production formulation of the same rule: the figure should fall **on the page of the reference or the facing page of the same spread** — the reader should never have to turn a leaf to see a figure under discussion. [LIKELY for the exact "or facing page" wording as a general trade rule; VERIFIED for the CMoS same-page/soon-after rule.]
- **Self-sufficiency** [LIKELY — standard in scientific and technical publishing; reinforced by Tufte and Schriver]: a figure plus its caption should be intelligible without hunting through body text — captions state what the figure shows and what to see in it, since many readers enter the page through the figures. Schriver's taxonomy [VERIFIED] gives the diagnostic: the caption should establish a *complementary* or *supplementary* relation to the figure, not merely repeat a title (empty redundancy) and not leave the relation unstated.
- **Integration** [VERIFIED, secondary] Tufte's *Beautiful Evidence* argument applies directly: words, numbers, and images belong together at the point of argument; a figure exiled from its discussion (or a caption exiled from its figure) is a production artifact, not a reasoning aid. Labels directly on the image beat legends that force back-and-forth lookup [LIKELY — consistent Tufte/Few doctrine].

## T5. Emphasis discipline

- [VERIFIED — Penguin rules] The classical book standard: *italic* is the emphasis form for text matter ("Italics are to be used for emphasis, for foreign words and phrases, and for the titles of books, newspapers, and plays"); and "Avoid bold faces" (stated for preliminary pages; bold appears nowhere in the Rules as a text-emphasis option). Small caps are structural (running heads, contents, bibliographic author names), always slightly letterspaced.
- [VERIFIED — Butterick] "Bold or italic — think of them as mutually exclusive"; use both "as little as possible"; "if everything is emphasized, then nothing is emphasized"; bold italic and underlined bold are "terrible ideas"; never underline. Caps acceptable for less than one line; small caps must be real, and letterspaced 5–12%.
- [VERIFIED — Bringhurst via TOC] Structural small-cap usage: spaced small caps for abbreviations/acronyms in text; text figures in prose so numerals don't shout.
- [LIKELY — Willberg/Forssman] Match emphasis type to reading mode: *integrated* emphasis (italic — invisible until reached) for linear reading; *active* emphasis (bold — findable from a distance) for informative and selective reading. Using bold inside a novel page or only italics inside a reference page are both mode errors.
- **How many levels before collapse:** [VERIFIED — Butterick's principle] emphasis is a scarcity economy. [LIKELY — general craft consensus, taught e.g. in Lynch & Horton's *Web Style Guide* as "change one parameter at a time"] each additional simultaneous signal (size + weight + caps + color) adds noise faster than meaning; practical ceiling in book work is two in-text emphasis forms (italic + bold, each with a distinct job) plus structural small caps — beyond that, hierarchy must come from position, size, and space, not from further character styling. [Attribution note: the "one parameter" phrasing is Web Style Guide's, not Bringhurst's — do not credit it to Bringhurst.]
- [VERIFIED, secondary — Lupton] The corresponding type crime: "too many signals" — redundant cues stacked on one hierarchical level [LIKELY for the exact label].

## T6. Sidebars, pull quotes, and boxes

- **When they aid**: [inference anchored in VERIFIED frameworks] Sidebars institutionalize Willberg/Forssman's *selektierendes Lesen* — they work when the book is genuinely meant to be read selectively (textbooks, manuals, guidebooks) and when the boxed matter is a distinct content *level* (procedure, example, warning, definition) that the typography consistently distinguishes. Tufte's practice [VERIFIED] shows the constructive form: margin material adjacent to, and in support of, the exact passage it annotates, "content-driven, rather than design-driven."
- **When they fragment**: the **seductive details effect** [VERIFIED via multiple research sources] — since the late 1980s (Garner et al.), educational-psychology studies show that "interesting digressions that relate superficially to the learning topic" (fun facts, anecdotes, decorative pictures — the classic textbook box) reliably *reduce* retention and transfer. Harp & Mayer (1998) identified the mechanisms (distraction, disruption, diversion — with diversion best supported); damage is worst when seductive details appear **early or interspersed** rather than grouped or late; Jaeger & Wiley found decorative images also impair readers' ability to monitor their own comprehension; Wesenberg et al. (2025) found grouping seductive details together mitigates harm versus interspersing them.
  - Rubric translation: a box must pass a relevance test (does it serve the chapter's learning goal, or merely its "appeal"?), a placement test (not interrupting the argument mid-stream), and a signaling test (typography must make its optionality obvious).
- **Pull quotes** [LIKELY — general editorial practice; no strong research located this pass]: native to magazine/informative reading, where they serve scanning and entry-point creation (a *redundant* word-word relation, in Schriver's terms — deliberate repetition as bait). In continuous trade nonfiction they interrupt linear reading and spoil upcoming text; standard advice restricts them to genuinely skim-oriented layouts. [UNCERTAIN] whether any controlled study measures pull-quote effects on book-length reading; none was found.
- **Schriver's relevance** [VERIFIED for the underlying findings]: her research program treats page elements as answers to readers' questions; boxed matter that no reader task summons is clutter, and her blame-study result warns that readers won't report the failure — they'll absorb it.

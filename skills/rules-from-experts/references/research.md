## Naming the discipline

The person usually cannot search for help because they do not know the word. Supplying it is often the highest-value thing in the whole exercise.

**Search, do not recall.** Terminology drifts, fields rename themselves, and the term used in academic work is frequently not the term practitioners use. Two or three searches settle it.

Useful search shapes:

- `"<their description of the symptom>" design principles` — finds the practitioner term
- `<candidate term> definition guidelines <field>` — confirms or kills a candidate
- `<candidate term> vs <adjacent term>` — finds where the boundary actually sits

**Deliver four things:**

1. **Umbrella term.** What to put in a book search or a job description.
2. **Precise term for their symptom.** Narrower, more useful. Someone complaining that sections blur together has a *grouping failure*, which is far more searchable than "layout problems".
3. **A layer map.** Most craft topics decompose into two to five layers that fail differently. Give a table: layer, name, the question it answers. This is what lets the person tell which of their problems is which.
4. **Adjacent terms.** Things they will meet and should be able to place.

**Watch for a conflation.** Frequently the person has merged two crafts under one complaint. Naming both, and stating that they fail differently and are fixed differently, is often the reframe that makes the rest of the work land.

## Finding named individuals

An organization is not an expert. Find the people inside it who wrote the relevant work — a reader needs a name to search for, and different researchers within one institution often hold different positions.

Search shapes that surface names:

- `<topic> "principles" OR "guidelines" author book`
- `<topic> research <institution>` then read the bylines
- `<known expert name> influenced by OR cites` — walks you up the citation chain
- `<topic> classic book` — finds the canon
- `<topic> benchmark study data` — finds the empirical tradition

**Walk backwards from the practitioner literature to the research.** Practitioner articles cite their sources. Following those citations one or two hops reliably lands you in the foundational tradition, which is where the mechanisms live.

**Never invent a name.** If you cannot point to the search result a name came from, cut it. A confident fabricated attribution is the single worst failure mode of this pipeline, and it is not recoverable — it poisons the reader's trust in every other claim.

**Include institutional sources honestly.** Design systems, standards bodies, and government guidance are authoritative and enforceable but are not individuals. Put them in their own tier and say plainly that they are not experts in the same sense.

## The four traditions

Nearly every craft topic has these, and they rarely cite each other. Finding all of them is what separates a real roster from a reading list.

| Tradition | Answers | Blind spot |
|---|---|---|
| **Foundational research** | Why does this work at all? What is the mechanism? | Old; assumes conditions that no longer hold; not written for practitioners |
| **The canon** | What is the shared vocabulary and the reusable pattern set? | Descriptive rather than tested; some patterns are dated |
| **Empirical and applied** | What actually breaks in front of real people? | Usually concentrated in one domain and over-generalized |
| **Practitioners and systems** | What do I type today? | Opinion presented as finding; little evidence |

For each tradition in the roster, state what it answers and where it is weak. This does two things: it tells the reader how much weight to give each source, and it sets up Phase 5, because disagreements almost always run along tradition lines.

## Source quality

Ranked, best first:

1. **Primary texts** — the book, the paper, the original article by the person themselves.
2. **Institutional research** — published benchmarks, eye-tracking studies, standards documents.
3. **The author's own secondary writing** — blog posts, talks, checklists by the same person.
4. **Reputable summaries** — useful for finding the primary source, not for quoting.
5. **Aggregator and SEO content** — use only to discover names, never as evidence.

**Fetch, do not rely on snippets.** A search snippet gives you a fragment out of context and often inverts a qualified claim into an absolute one. Open the page for anything doing real work in your report.

**Prefer the source's own numbers.** Secondary write-ups round, drop conditions, and occasionally invent. If a number matters, get it from the source.

## What a good deep dive contains

Per expert or tight cluster:

**Primary text with year.** Specific. "Their 2009 book" is not enough; name it.

**The actual mechanism.** Not "they say hierarchy matters" but *how* they say it is produced and *why* that works. The mechanism is what transfers to new situations; the slogan is not.

**Their numbers.** Thresholds, limits, percentages, measured effects, sample sizes. This is the most common thing a shallow run omits and the most useful thing a reader gets. If a source says no more than three of something, that is a rule you can enforce; "keep it simple" is not.

**Their caveat.** Serious sources qualify themselves. A source presented without its own qualification has been misrepresented.

**The practical consequence.** One or two sentences connecting the claim to the person's actual problem. Without this the report is a literature review.

**Where they are wrong or dated.** Foundational work often assumes conditions that no longer hold. Saying so is not disrespect; it is what makes the report usable.

## Citation and copyright discipline

- **Paraphrase by default.** Rewrite fully in your own words — not the same sentence with synonyms.
- **Quote only when exact wording changes the meaning.** A definition, a coined term, a claim whose force lives in its phrasing.
- **Keep every quote under fifteen words, and one quote per source at most.**
- **Never reproduce long passages, and never reconstruct a source's structure** section by section.
- **Cite what you retrieved.** Author, work, year, and a link where one exists.
- **Attribute precisely.** If a finding is from one researcher at an institution, name the researcher rather than the institution.

## Handling conflicts and gaps

**When sources conflict**, that is signal, not noise. Record both positions with attribution and carry them into Phase 5. Do not average them into a bland middle.

**When evidence is thin**, say so in the report where the claim appears, not only in a preface. Useful flags:

- The finding comes from one domain and is being generalized here.
- The claim is descriptive vocabulary rather than a measured result.
- Attempts to replicate it have been mixed.
- No comparable large study exists for this context.

A report that marks its own weak spots is more useful than one that presents everything at equal confidence, and it is the thing a knowledgeable reader checks for first.

**When you cannot find something**, say that too. "There is no large-scale benchmark for this" is a real finding and often explains why the person's team keeps arguing about it.

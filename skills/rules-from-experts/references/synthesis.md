## Why this phase exists

A report that lists what everyone thinks is a reading list. The person cannot act on it, because the moment they try, they hit a place where two respected sources say opposite things and they have no basis to choose.

This phase is where the work becomes usable. It is also the phase most often skipped, because it is the only one that requires committing to a view.

**The diagnostic: if you found no disagreements, you have not researched deeply enough.** Experts in any mature field disagree, often sharply. Apparent harmony means you read secondary summaries, which sand off exactly the edges that matter. Go back to Phase 4 and read the primary sources.

## Section 1: Consensus

Points where every tradition converges independently. These carry unusual weight precisely because the traditions do not cite each other — when a perception researcher, a benchmark study, and a working practitioner arrive at the same rule by different routes, it is probably true.

Format each as:

1. **The claim**, stated as an instruction or an assertion.
2. **Who arrives at it, and from what direction.** Naming the different routes is what demonstrates convergence rather than repetition.

Five to eight consensus points is typical. These become the MUST and NEVER rules in Phase 6, and it is worth saying plainly in the report that violations of these are defects rather than debates.

## Section 2: Live disagreements

For each disagreement, use this shape:

**A table of positions**

| Position | Who holds it | Their argument |
|---|---|---|

Two to four rows. State each argument at its strongest — a disagreement summarized unfairly is not resolved, it is dismissed.

**A resolution**

Then a paragraph beginning **Resolution**, which does exactly one of two things:

- Shows the disagreement is not real, and says precisely why, or
- Accepts that it is real, picks a side, and gives the reasoning.

What it must never do is say "it depends" and stop. If it depends, say **what** it depends on, then give the decision rule keyed to that variable. A decision rule the reader can apply is the deliverable.

Three to five disagreements is typical for a standard craft topic.

## The six resolution moves

Most disagreements dissolve under one of these. Naming which move you are making keeps the resolution honest.

**1. Different questions.** The two sides appear to conflict but answer different things. One is talking about how many levels; the other about how large each step should be. Both are right; the reader needs both.

**2. Different scope.** One argues about the whole system, the other about a single component. Or one about the count of a thing, the other about its intensity. State the scopes and the conflict evaporates.

**3. Different constraints.** One assumes conditions the other does not — abundant space, fast systems, expert users, a controlled catalog. The rule then keys to the constraint: use position A when the constraint holds, position B when it does not.

**4. Superseded.** One position assumed something that is no longer true. Say what changed and which part of the old position survives. Foundational work is frequently right about the mechanism and wrong about the implementation.

**5. Genuinely live — pick.** Sometimes there is a real, unresolved disagreement between competent people. Say so, pick the side you would ship, and give your reasoning. Note what would change your mind. This is more useful than neutrality and more honest than pretending the question is settled.

**6. Both, keyed to a variable.** The most common outcome for practical questions. Neither side is wrong; the answer keys to something measurable — volume, frequency, user expertise, latency, screen size. Produce a small decision table keyed to that variable. This is the highest-value resolution shape, because it converts an argument into a lookup.

## Taking a position

The report earns its keep by being willing to say what to do. Guidance:

**Prefer the empirical over the elegant** when they conflict and the test conditions actually match the person's situation.

**Prefer the mechanism over the rule of thumb** when transferring to a new context. Rules of thumb are compressed for one setting; mechanisms survive the move.

**Distrust unanimity among practitioners** when no research supports it. Practitioner consensus often tracks fashion and tooling.

**Distrust research** whose conditions do not resemble the person's situation. A laboratory finding under ideal conditions may not survive a slow network and a distracted user.

**Say when your position is weakly held.** Marking confidence lets the reader weigh it. A position stated with false certainty is worse than a hedged one, because it cannot be revised.

---

## The closing synthesis

End the report with a one-page section: five to eight numbered claims that carry the whole thing.

Each claim should be a full sentence stating a position, not a topic label. "Attention is a fixed budget, and every emphasized element spends from the same pool" is a claim. "Attention" is not.

This section is the bridge to Phase 6. If a claim here cannot be turned into rules, it is too abstract — sharpen it. If a rule in Phase 6 does not trace back to a claim here, it came from nowhere and should be cut or the synthesis should be extended to cover it.
